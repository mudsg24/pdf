### Fig_70_2

![Fig_70_2](Fig_70_2.png)

Fig. 70.2 Stages of T-cell activation: multiple targets for immunosuppressive agents. Signal 1: The Ca²⁺-dependent signal induced by T-cell receptor (TCR) stimulation results in calcineurin activation, a process inhibited by the calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs). Calcineurin dephosphorylates nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), enabling it to enter the nucleus and bind to the interleukin-2 (IL-2) promoter. Corticosteroids bind to cytoplasmic receptors and the nuclear receptor-steroid complex binds to glucocorticoid response elements present in the promoter of many genes. Corticosteroids also inhibit activation of the transcription factor, nuclear factor-κB (not shown). Signal 2: Costimulatory signals, such as that between CD28 on the T cell and B7 on the APC, are necessary to optimize T-cell transcription of the IL-2 gene, prevent T-cell anergy, and inhibit T-cell apoptosis. Signal 3: IL-2 receptor stimulation induces the T cell to enter the cell cycle and proliferate. IL-2 and related cytokines have both autocrine and paracrine effects. Signal 3 may be blocked by IL-2 receptor antibodies or by sirolimus. Further downstream, Azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) inhibit progression into the cell cycle by inhibiting purine and therefore DNA synthesis. (From Halloran PF: Immunosuppressive drugs for kidney transplantation. N Engl J Med 351:2715-2729, 2004.)

### Fig_70_3

![Fig_70_3](Fig_70_3.png)

Fig. 70.3 Management of kidney allograft non-function/oliguria immediately after transplant. A flowchart showing the decision process for a non-functioning or oliguric kidney allograft. It starts with assessing for likely technical causes or hyperacute rejection. If yes, appropriate specific intervention is needed. If no, assess volume status. If volume depleted, give a fluid challenge. If volume overloaded, give furosemide. If euvolemic, give a fluid challenge with a diuretic. If there is still no diuresis, perform an ultrasound, reassess crossmatch, and consider an urgent DSA screen/biopsy if high immunological risk, and evaluate the need for dialysis.

### Fig_70_4

![Fig_70_4](Fig_70_4.png)

Fig. 70.4 Algorithm for management of persistent delayed graft function. A flowchart showing management of persistent DGF. It splits based on high-risk vs low-risk recipients. High-risk recipients get a biopsy at day 3-5. Low-risk recipients get a biopsy at day 7-10. If there is no rejection, a re-biopsy is done in 7-10 days if DGF persists. If there is rejection, the path splits into Acute ABMR, Mild/moderate TCMR, and Severe TCMR. ABMR is treated with Pulse steroids, Plasmapheresis, IVIg, Rituximab. Mild/moderate TCMR is treated with a steroid pulse. Severe TCMR is treated with a steroid pulse and thymoglobulin. The presence of anti-donor human leukocyte antigen (HLA) antibodies should prompt immediate biopsy in this setting.

### Fig_70_5

![Fig_70_5](Fig_70_5.png)

Fig. 70.5 Algorithm for management of allograft dysfunction in early posttransplant period. A flowchart for managing elevated creatinine. First, exclude/treat volume depletion, TMA, and pyelonephritis, and use imaging to rule out urological/vascular causes. Then, check cyclosporine/tacrolimus trough blood concentration. If CNI levels are high, reduce the dose and re-evaluate in 2-4 days. If no improvement, proceed to biopsy. If CNI levels are not elevated, proceed to biopsy. If biopsy is high risk/unavailable, an empiric steroid boost can be given. If there's no response in 3-5 days, a biopsy is recommended. The biopsy leads to specific intervention. CNI, Calcineurin inhibitor.

### Fig_70_6

![Fig_70_6](Fig_70_6.png)

Fig. 70.6 The multifactorial pathogenesis of chronic allograft injury. A diagram illustrates factors leading to allograft failure. Donor kidney disease and ischemic injury affect the donor kidney. The recipient contributes factors like diabetes, hypertension, and vascular disease. After transplant, CNI nephrotoxicity and chronic active ABMR contribute to nephron loss, along with events like acute TCMR, active ABMR, BK nephritis, and recurrent GN. All these factors converge on nephron loss, ultimately causing allograft failure.
