### Fig_15_1

![Fig_15_1](Fig_15_1.png)

Fig. 15.1 HCO <sup>−</sup> reabsorption along the nephron. The proximal tubule reabsorbs approximately 80% of the filtered HCO <sup>−</sup>. Substantia amounts are also reabsorbed in the thick ascending limb. The remaining amounts (∼5%) are reabsorbed in the collecting duct, which is critical for final regulation of acid excretion. The mechanisms are described in Chapter 9, but the main apical transporters (Na-H exchange and H<sup>+</sup>-ATPase) are illustrated. Little HCO <sup>−</sup> (∼0%) remains in the final urine.

### Fig_15_3

![Fig_15_3](Fig_15_3.png)

Fig. 15.3 Synchrony of regulation of ammonium production (from glutamine [GLN] precursors, and excretion). Process allows gener- ation of “new” HCO <sup>−</sup> by the kidney. NH <sup>+</sup> excretion is regulated in response to changes in systemic acid-base and K<sup>+</sup> balance. Contributing segments include the proximal convoluted tubule, proximal straight tubule, thin descending limb, thick ascending limb, and medullary collecting duct. Upregulated by acidosis and hypokalemia. Inhibited by hyperkalemia.

### Fig_15_7

![Fig_15_7](Fig_15_7.png)

Fig. 15.7 Cell models of ammonia synthesis and excretion pathways. (A) Proximal convoluted tubule. Ammonia is derived from glutamine precursors to produce two NH <sup>+</sup> and two HCO <sup>−</sup> molecules through an enzymatic pathway activated by acidemia and hypokalemia and inhibited by alkalemia and hyperkalemia. (B) Type A intercalated cell in collecting tubule. Ammonium entry across basolateral membrane through sub- stitution of NH <sup>+</sup> for K<sup>+</sup> on K<sup>+</sup> conductance and secreted across apical membrane via ROMK or RhCG (see text). In both A and B, NH diffusion coupled with H<sup>+</sup> secretion traps NH <sup>+</sup> in the tubule lumen.

### Fig_15_8

![Fig_15_8](Fig_15_8.png)

Fig. 15.8 Diagram of some of the renal interactions of acid-base status and K<sup>+</sup>. Proximal tubule cell, a collecting duct principal cell (PC), and intercalated cell (IC) are shown. Acidosis (↓ pH) causes renal potassium retention by several actions including inhibiting secretory K<sup>+</sup> channels, stimulating H<sup>+</sup>-K<sup>+</sup>-ATPase, which reabsorbs K<sup>+</sup>, and in- creasing ammoniagenesis, which may have secondary actions that block K<sup>+</sup> secretion. Alkalosis can have the reverse effects. The effects also work in the other direction: Potassium depletion (↓ K<sup>+</sup>) stimulates ammoniagenesis and H<sup>+</sup> secretion/bicarbonate reabsorption, as well as distal acid secretion, all of which may cause metabolic alkalosis. Hyperkalemia can have some of the opposite effects, such as inhib- iting thick ascending limb ammonium transport and inhibiting urinary ammonium excretion, an effect that may cause acidosis. (From Hamm LL, Hering-Smith KS, Nakhoul NL. Acid-base and potassium homeosta- sis. Semin Nephrol. 2013;333:257–264.)
